## You Paid to Get Them in the Door—So Why Is Treating Everyone Equally Draining Your Profit?

In FY2026, **Costco** sold **$297.25 billion** worth of merchandise at a razor-thin **11.09%** product gross margin—yet finished the year with **$9.23 billion** in net income. In Q3 2026, **Duolingo** climbed to **153.1 million** monthly active users, more than **91%** of whom never spent a cent—yet the company still posted a **72.9%** quarterly gross margin. And in **Apple's** FY2026, software and services made up just **25.8%** of total revenue—yet carried **40.2%** of full-year gross profit.

Those three freshly filed numbers shred the conventional wisdom many founders have run on for years. Most consumer brands and e-commerce operators live trapped in the same exhausting loop: trying to squeeze five extra margin points out of every single order on Monday, then panicking over slow traffic on Friday and hanging "20% Off Sitewide" banners across the homepage.

In [the five gears of brand growth](/en/essays/five-gears-of-brand-growth/), we mapped out the five moving parts behind any lasting consumer business. In [our previous essay on positioning, channels, and brand extensions](/en/essays/positioning-channels-brand-extension-nike-on-lego/), we used Nike, On Running, and LEGO to unpack the first gear—picking the right arena and the right shelves. Once your product is on the shelf and shoppers are walking through the door, you hit the second gear, where real operator discipline shows up: **who you actually build for, and how you price.**

Most brands work harder every quarter while keeping less cash—not because the product is weak, but because they run the ledger backward:

- **Obsessing over order one while ignoring years two through five.** You squeeze an extra ten dollars out of a first-time checkout and scare off someone who would have come back ten times.
- **Treating every shopper like a clone.** A loyal regular who buys five times a year and a bargain hunter who only clicks during holiday clearance get the exact same coupon and service—so your real profit quietly subsidizes people who never return.
- **Using markdowns to mask lazy pricing.** Keep slapping red sale tags on a solid product, and you train the market to wait for a deal before pulling out a credit card.

The companies that turn repeat profit into clockwork do the exact opposite. Lay the late-2026 filings of **Costco**, **Duolingo**, and **Apple** side by side on a desk. One sells bulk groceries, one teaches languages, and one builds flagship hardware. Underneath, all three play the exact same hand:

> **Know who actually pays the bills, build clean tiers for different budgets, and defend the full-price yardstick sitting inside your customer's head.**

## 1. Costco's Membership Ledger: 11.09% Product Margin, $9.23 Billion in Net Income

The first time most e-commerce founders open a Costco annual report, the numbers look broken. For FY2026 (ended **August 30, 2026**), Costco generated **$303.15 billion** in total revenue, including **$297.25 billion** in net merchandise sales (up **10.1%** year over year). Across that massive retail footprint, full-year merchandise gross margin sat at just **11.09%**.

Costco enforces hard internal markup caps: roughly **14%** on national brands and around **15%** on its private-label **Kirkland Signature** line, pricing everyday goods **15% to 20%** below comparable big-name alternatives. For a typical retailer or direct-to-consumer store, an 11% product margin wouldn't even cover ad bills and fulfillment costs—you'd be out of business inside a year.

Yet Costco quietly cleared **$9.23 billion** in net income. Where did the profit come from? Look one line below merchandise sales: in FY2026, Costco collected **$5.91 billion** in membership fees (up **11%** year over year). Those fees accounted for **under 2%** of total revenue, required virtually zero extra inventory or shipping spend, and equaled **64%** of full-year net income.

Charging an annual fee, however, is only the surface. What competitors still struggle to copy is how cleanly Costco separates the people walking through its aisles. By the end of FY2026, Costco had **84.1 million** paid household memberships worldwide across just two tiers: standard **Gold Star** at **$65** a year, and **Executive** at **$130** a year with a **2%** annual reward on eligible purchases (capped at **$1,250**).

Now look at the split buried inside those 84.1 million households. Roughly half—**42.3 million** households (**50.3%**)—hold Executive memberships. Yet that half alone drove **75.2%** of worldwide net sales. Tap those numbers into a calculator and the gap jumps off the page: **an Executive member spends roughly 3x as much per year as a standard member.**

Then add retention. In FY2026, renewal rates hit **92.3%** in the US and Canada and **89.8%** worldwide. Out of every ten households that join, nine hand over their annual fee again the very next year.

With that ledger in front of you, Costco's famous **$1.50** quarter-pound hot dog and soda combo makes complete sense. Costco hasn't raised that price in roughly **40 years**, even building its own hot-dog processing plants to hold costs flat. That combo was never meant to earn food-court margin. It is a living billboard parked right at the front entrance: shoppers bite into a $1.50 hot dog, decide on the spot that this store never gouges them, and happily roll a $300 cart toward checkout.

Countless brands stumble because they treat every customer the same—pouring subsidies onto casual deal-browsers while taking dependable regulars for granted. Costco flips the math. The annual fee filters out pure window-shoppers, and the "$65 more for 2% back" rule lets high-frequency households raise their own hands and step into the Executive pool. Visit twice a year, and $130 feels steep. Shop every week, and your annual reward pays back the upgrade fee with cash left over.

Once that math clicks, the entire business shifts. Costco couldn't care less whether today's pack of paper towels earns a dollar or fifty cents. It is running a ten-year ledger on a high-spending household's fees and compounding basket size. Customers are never one uniform block; your first job as an operator is using clear rules to surface the regulars worth three average buyers—and putting your best perks directly behind keeping them.

## 2. Duolingo's Free-User Math: 91%+ Never Pay—So Why Did It Retire Its Priciest Tier?

Now swing from physical warehouse clubs to the exact opposite corner of consumer business: the mobile language app **Duolingo**. Where Costco puts a tollbooth at the front door, Duolingo lets more than nine out of ten people use the product for free.

Look at Duolingo's **Q3 2026** shareholder letter and earnings release (published **November 3, 2026**): monthly active users reached **153.1 million** (up **13%** year over year), while daily active users surged to **64.9 million** (up **29%** year over year, accelerating past Q2's **23%** pace). Its daily-to-monthly active ratio (**DAU/MAU**) climbed to **42.4%**, and active-learner retention hit a record **~84%**.

Out of that huge crowd, how many actually pay? Just **13.1 million** subscribers (up **14%** year over year). Put plainly, out of every 100 monthly users, roughly **8.6** open their wallets—and **over 91%** never pay a dime. Plenty of anxious executives would call an emergency meeting on the spot: *"Look at all these freeloaders—put up a strict paywall and lock the app after three days!"*

Thankfully, Duolingo didn't take the bait. Running off that sub-10% core of paying fans alone, Q3 2026 revenue reached **$309 million** (up **14%** year over year), gross margin hit **72.9%**, and Adjusted EBITDA climbed to **$78.7 million** (**25.5%** margin).

Why refuse to force the 91% into paying upfront? Because two shoppers staring at the exact same product rarely bring the same budget. A curious first-timer downloading an app starts with a mental budget of zero. Hit them with a mandatory checkout screen on day one, and they don't convert into paying customers—they delete the app.

Duolingo saw a bigger ledger. Those 91% free learners may not pay cash, yet they erase the heaviest cash burn in consumer business: **sky-high paid ad acquisition**. Most of Duolingo's growth rolls in organically at zero media spend as free players protect daily streaks, share memes across social media (**over 1 billion** social impressions in a single quarter), and discover the brand through gaming partnerships like *Brawl Stars* and *Eggy Party*. The free layer grows the pool and builds the daily habit; the paid tier waits patiently downstream for committed learners who hate ads, dread breaking a streak, and want faster progress.

Even more instructive for anyone setting prices is the surgical tier move Duolingo executed in late 2026.

When Duolingo first launched AI Video Call speaking practice, the compute behind each session ran roughly **$0.30**. Folding an expensive feature like that into the core subscription would have torn right through gross margins. So Duolingo fenced it inside a premium top tier—**Duolingo Max**—letting eager power users shoulder the early cost. At that point, Max accounted for only a single-digit share of bookings.

By late 2026, the underlying cost curve fell off a cliff. Moving onto open-weight AI models slashed per-call cost by **over 96%**, down to **under $0.01**. A short-sighted management team would have kept quiet, held the luxury price tag steady, and pocketed the windfall.

Duolingo did the opposite: **it retired the standalone Max tier**, moved unlimited AI Video Call practice straight into its mainstream paid tier (**Super Duolingo**) and Family plan, and smoothly upgraded existing individual Max subscribers into the Family plan at the same price. Overnight, the number of paying subscribers with access to AI speaking practice expanded roughly **10x**. The flagship plan became dramatically more valuable, renewals locked in tighter, and overall gross margin still held rock-steady at **72.9%**.

That single decision lays down two iron rules for every operator:

> **Smart pricing never traps every buyer inside a single take-it-or-leave-it sticker. It gives cautious newcomers an easy way in, lets heavy users step up on their own—and the moment technology or supply-chain costs drop sharply, passes yesterday's luxury feature down into the flagship tier, trading a brief margin spike for years of rock-solid renewals.**

## 3. Apple's Tiered Pricing Playbook: Zero Official Flagship Discounts, 40.2% of Gross Profit from Services

Right around here, founders selling physical goods usually push back: *"Costco has warehouse scale, and Duolingo sells software where one more user costs almost nothing. I sell tangible hardware and consumer products. Price too high and newcomers walk away; run sales to move stock and regulars get angry and stop buying full price. How do I solve that?"*

Open **Apple's** FY2026 annual report (filed **November 3, 2026**, for the fiscal year ended **September 26, 2026**). Full-year revenue set a new record at **$478.83 billion** (up **15%** year over year), consolidated gross margin reached **48.8%**, and total gross profit hit **$233.60 billion**.

Split that revenue down the middle, and you see one of the widest profit spreads in global business. Hardware products brought in **$355.53 billion** (**74.2%** of revenue), producing **$139.66 billion** in gross profit at a **39.3%** margin. Software and services—spanning iCloud, the App Store, paid subscriptions, and more—brought in just **$123.30 billion** (**25.8%** of revenue), yet delivered **$93.94 billion** in gross profit at a staggering **76.2%** margin.

Do the plain math: **a Services arm representing barely a quarter of revenue carries more than four-tenths (40.2%) of Apple's total gross profit**—all built atop an installed base of **over 2.5 billion** active devices and **more than 1.5 billion** paid subscriptions.

How does Apple collect steep premiums at the top end while steadily bringing budget-conscious newcomers into that 2.5-billion-device ecosystem? It relies on two disciplined plays.

**First, lift profit upward through clean configuration steps—never blunt, across-the-board hikes.** Look at US retail pricing for the **iPhone 18 Pro**, unveiled on **September 9, 2026**: the 256GB base model starts at **$1,199**; stepping up to 512GB adds **$200** (**$1,399**); jumping to 1TB adds **$400** (**$1,799**); and the top 2TB spec reaches **$2,399**—a full **$1,200** above base. The **iPhone 18 Pro Max** sits **$100** higher at every single step, running from **$1,299** up to **$2,499**.

Study that storage ladder for a second. The R&D, display, silicon, and chassis are identical across every box; extra flash storage costs nowhere near $1,200 to manufacture. Yet Apple doesn't slap a flat $1,799 tag on every phone and price out mainstream buyers. Tighter budgets still walk through the door at $1,199, while heavy video creators who hate deleting files happily step up to 1TB or 2TB and lift unit profit to the ceiling.

**Second, catch tighter budgets downward without ever cracking the full-price yardstick.** Browse Apple.com and notice what is missing: practically zero flashing red banners announcing "30% Off Sitewide" or "$300 Flash Sale."

Why defend the flagship price tag so fiercely? Because the moment a brand officially marks its hero product down 20% in June and 30% in November, the yardstick inside the buyer's mind slides from $1,199 down to $899—and never springs back. From that day forward, full price feels like getting overcharged: *"Why pay full freight today? Just wait for the next promo."* Countless promising consumer and e-commerce brands didn't get beaten by rivals—they broke their own full-price yardstick with self-inflicted discounts.

Does Apple walk away from tighter budgets? Not for a second. Every new buyer entering that 2.5-billion-device pool generates years of cloud storage, App Store fees, and subscriptions. Instead of cutting the flagship sticker, Apple lowers entry friction with **five price-safe tools: keeping prior-generation models on sale, official trade-in credits, carrier subsidies, 0% interest installments, and seasonal gift-card promos**.

> **Your sticker price is the yardstick stored in your buyer's mind. Straight markdowns announce that your product was never worth full price to begin with. Trade-ins, 0% installments, and prior-generation entry models solve cash flow instead—welcoming new buyers through the door while the $1,199 flagship yardstick never budges an inch.**

## 5. Four Checks for Monday Morning: Fix Your Customer & Pricing Ledger in 14 Days

Billion-dollar earnings reports are fun to dissect, yet useless until they hit your own spreadsheet. Whether you run a direct-to-consumer store, an Amazon brand, or a physical retail line, sit down with your team Monday morning, audit these four ledgers, and run a focused 14-day test to see how much profit is quietly leaking out.

### 1. Pull a Three-Tier Customer Report and Find Out Who Actually Pays the Bills

Export the past 12 months of orders and sort every buyer by cumulative spend into three buckets: **top 20%**, **middle 30%**, and **bottom 50%**. Calculate each group's share of total revenue, net profit, and average days between repeat orders.

The numbers almost always jolt founders awake: the lion's share of real net profit sits inside that top 20% to 30% of repeat buyers. Starting next week, concentrate early product access, priority support, and repeat-buyer rewards squarely on them—and stop spraying no-minimum coupons at one-time bargain hunters who only ever bought a loss-leader item.

### 2. Build a Three-Step Tier Ladder on Your Hero Product—Ditch the Lazy Flat Price

Pick your top-selling hero product and run a 14-day test on the product page and cart with three clean options: an **entry tier** that removes friction for first-time buyers; a **core flagship tier** delivering unmistakable everyday value for the majority; and a **premium tier or bundle** (larger capacity, durable refills, extended warranty, or gift-ready set) built for time-strapped, heavy-usage buyers who gladly pay more to skip hassle. Remember: a top tier is never fluff—like Apple's storage ladder, it removes a concrete pain point your heaviest users genuinely care about.

### 3. Pull Down the Sitewide Sale Banners and Switch to Price-Safe Closing Tools

For the next 14 days, take down the "20% Off Sitewide" and "Flash Price Drop" banners on your storefront and hold the line on your flagship sticker price. When you need conversion lift or new-buyer velocity, swap in four levers that leave your headline price untouched: **a genuinely useful bonus accessory with flagship orders, referral credits for regulars, multi-unit bundle savings, and 0% installments or prior-generation models as an entry option.** Giving away a tangible add-on beats smashing your own full-price yardstick every single time.

### 4. Add Three Columns to Every Acquisition Channel Sheet: The 12-Month Repeat Ledger

At next week's performance review, stop pounding the table over first-order margin alone. Put every acquisition channel on one sheet and add three mandatory columns right next to order-one stats: **cumulative repeat revenue per customer group at Day 90, Day 180, and Day 365**, plus net profit after ad spend and fulfillment. Once that sheet sits flat on the table, the mirage disappears—you see immediately which loud, busy channels bleed cash over a year, and which channels look expensive on day one yet quietly keep sending you Executive-grade regulars month after month.

Once you separate your true regulars from bargain browsers and lock down disciplined pricing, an even sneakier trap usually hits next: ad spend climbs, conversion dashboards glow bright green, yet your month-end bank balance hasn't moved an inch.
