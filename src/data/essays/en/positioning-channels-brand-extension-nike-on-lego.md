## Same Running Shoes, 24-Point Margin Gap

Put the second-half 2026 earnings reports of **Nike** and **On Running** side by side on a desk, and one pair of numbers will stop you cold.

Both companies sell athletic running shoes. Both chase the same American shoppers strolling malls and scrolling social feeds. Both operate under the exact same US tariff regime. Yet once you strip out one-time 2026 tariff refunds, their **underlying gross margins** split wide open:

* **Nike** posted a **42.8%** gross margin in Q1 FY27 (ended late August 2026).
* **On Running** hit a record **66.9%** underlying gross margin in Q3 2026 (ended late September 2026)—and **72.6%** on a reported basis once tariff refunds are included.

Both sell shoes people lace onto their feet. So why does one company keep nearly **$67** in gross profit on every **$100** pair sold, while the other keeps less than **$43**?

The answer isn't hiding in shoelace fabric or celebrity endorsements. That entire **24-percentage-point** gap lives inside the three crossroads where growing consumer brands most often lose their footing:

1. **Should you spread wider, or pull tighter?**
2. **How do you split direct-to-consumer and wholesale without fighting yourself—or blowing up your prices?**
3. **When your hero product hits a ceiling, how do you launch new products without watering down the brand?**

In the previous essay, we mapped out the five gears of brand growth. Today we open up the first gear, using real earnings from **Nike**, **On Running**, and **LEGO** across their worst slumps and sharpest peaks to settle all three questions.

---

## 1. Kill the 30-Year False Debate: Spread Wide or Pull Tight?

Bring up brand building in any conference room, and the table almost always splits into two camps.

**Camp one says pull tight and sharpen your edge.** They argue a real brand needs a point of view and the nerve to turn away the wrong buyers—protect your niche appeal, defend your price tag, and never turn yourself into mass-market filler just to chase quick sales.

**Camp two says spread wide and show up everywhere.** They point out that most shoppers aren't writing love letters to your brand philosophy. Scale comes down to two plain realities: **whether a shopper thinks of you the moment they need new shoes, and whether they can grab a pair effortlessly when they walk into a store or pull out their phone.** The wider you distribute, the more passersby bump into you—and the bigger your business gets.

Both arguments sound smart on a whiteboard. Both wreck real businesses when taken to the extreme:

* Follow only camp one—hiding inside your own website and tight circle while refusing mainstream retail—and ad costs climb every quarter while new buyers still don't know your name. You stay small forever.
* Follow only camp two—saying yes to every shelf and every promo event—and sure, your boxes sit everywhere. They also sit at 30% off across the internet. Nobody pays full price anymore, and you slowly turn into a generic commodity.

The truth is these two camps were never opposites. They are **two halves of the same operating discipline**:

> **Show up wide enough that the right shopper can spot you and buy you without friction the moment a need hits—yet guard the velvet rope hard enough that no retailer or side project ever drags your full-price reputation into the clearance bin.**

Sound like a nice slogan? Look at the tuition Nike paid—and the scorecard On Running posted—in 2026, and you'll see exactly what that sentence is worth.

## 2. The Channel Tug-of-War: Direct vs. Wholesale at Nike and On Running

### Nike's Lesson: Cutting Wholesale Didn't Save the Middleman's Cut—It Handed Your Shelves to Rivals

A few years ago, Nike launched an aggressive direct-to-consumer push. On a spreadsheet, the logic looked airtight: pull inventory back into Nike.com, the Nike apps, and Nike-owned stores; keep the margin retailers used to pocket; own the customer relationship directly.

So Nike **cut roughly 50% of its wholesale partners** worldwide—pulling back or walking away from department stores like Macy's, footwear chains like DSW, and scores of neighborhood specialty running shops, while slashing allocations for decades-long partner Foot Locker.

On paper, direct share climbed fast. Then the hangover arrived.

Multi-brand shoe stores don't leave prime walls bare when Nike packs up. Store managers filled Nike's eye-level real estate with **On Running** and **Hoka**. A few years earlier, an American shopper out on a Saturday tried on three comfortable pairs of runners, and two were Nike. Once Nike stepped back, the first pair they noticed—and laced up—was a thick-soled Hoka or a hollow-pod On.

**A physical retail shelf is never just a fulfillment pipe; it is a giant billboard you don't pay per click to rent.** Walk out of your shopper's everyday browsing path, and you effectively hand competitors your best spot for free.

Worse still, when direct growth started stalling, Nike leaned hard on its biggest heritage franchises—like the Dunk and Air Force 1—cranking up production and repeating promotions to plug the revenue gap. Overexposure stripped away scarcity, and discounts kept sinking deeper.

Nike's 2026 results put the bill in black and white:

* In **FY26** (ended late May 2026), Nike wholesale rebounded **6%** to **$27.5 billion**, while the heavily bet-on Nike Direct business **fell 6%** to **$17.7 billion**.
* By **Q1 FY27** (ended late August 2026), Nike Direct slid another **8%**, with digital direct dropping **13%** in constant currency.

Incoming CEO Elliott Hill had to make two moves that looked contradictory at first glance: mend fences with major retail partners like **Dick's Sporting Goods** and **Foot Locker** to win back lost running walls, while intentionally **cutting supply of classic franchises by roughly 20%**, accepting several quarters of top-line pain to flush bloated inventory.

### On Running's Counterpunch: Why It Hit the Wholesale Brakes at Peak Growth

Now look at **On Running** on the exact same battlefield. Plenty of people assume On earns a 66.9% underlying gross margin because it "only sells direct and skips physical stores." That couldn't be further from the truth.

On cares deeply about physical reach. Its global footprint already spans **11,000+ selective retail doors**, anchored in specialty running shops and prime placements inside the top experiential stores of **Dick's Sporting Goods**—the North American sporting-goods heavyweight that now commands **3,200+ stores** after combining with Foot Locker.

**What separates On from ordinary brands isn't whether it uses wholesale—it's having the nerve to say "no" once it gets on the shelf.**

In **Q2 2026**, the US sneaker market turned into a promotional bloodbath. On's direct-to-consumer business was roaring—up **26%** reported (**+34.3%** in constant currency), lifting direct mix to **45.7%**—and Americas wholesalers were hungry for more stock. Yet On's leadership made a call few public-company executives have the stomach for: **they deliberately tapped the brakes on Americas wholesale shipments**, letting Q2 wholesale growth slow to just **4.8%**.

On's co-CEO was blunt on the earnings call: markdowns were flying everywhere across US retail, and stuffing wholesale warehouses only tempts retailers to clear unsold pairs at a discount. On would rather leave one quarter of wholesale sales on the table than crack its full-price discipline, keeping retail shelves clean for upcoming flagship launches.

Three months later, **Q3 2026** results (reported in November 2026) delivered the verdict:

* Quarterly revenue hit a record **CHF 931.4 million (+17.2%)**;
* Direct-to-consumer jumped another **25%**;
* Wholesale rebounded cleanly to **+12.2%** growth because partner inventory stayed lean and full-price sell-through stayed brisk;
* Underlying gross margin (excluding tariff refunds) reached a record **66.9%**.

What gives On the clout to dictate terms to giant retail chains isn't slick ad copy. It is expensive, hard-to-copy product engineering—from its instantly recognizable hollow-pod sole to **LightSpray**, a seamless upper spun onto the shoe by robotic arms in **Zurich** (2025–2026). On doesn't blast breakthrough launches like that across every outlet online; it limits stock to its own flagship stores and top-tier specialty retailers whose staff know how to explain the craft.

Put those two playbooks side by side, and the split between your own store and wholesale becomes unmistakable:

* **Wholesale exists to get you discovered, tried on, and trusted by everyday shoppers**—provided you pick partners that fit your standard, won't wreck your pricing, and accept shipments controlled like a water valve. Running slightly lean on store shelves beats watching your hero product pile up on a clearance rack every time.
* **Direct—your website, apps, and flagship stores—exists to tell the full product story, launch your best work first, and compound repeat buyers.** It is your full-price lighthouse, never an online outlet bin.

## 3. The Scaling Trap: How to Launch New Products Without Blurring the Brand

Once channels are sorted, growing brands run straight into a second wall: your hero product is approaching a ceiling, you want new lines and new customers—so why do some brands expand into giants while others launch one new category and suddenly feel foreign to their own fans?

Remarkably, business history's sharpest cautionary tale and its cleanest comeback belong to the exact same company: **LEGO**.

### The Warning Sign (2003): Chasing Trends Until Nobody Recognizes You

Today's dominance makes it easy to forget that in **2003**, LEGO stood inches from bankruptcy, bleeding roughly **$1 million a day**.

The anxiety felt eerily modern: video games were exploding, and classic plastic bricks looked sluggish. Leadership panicked and raced into "brand extensions"—pouring money into theme parks, kids' clothing, wristwatches, girls' jewelry (**Clikits**), and an action-figure line called *Galidor*.

The fatal mistake? **The Galidor action-figure parts didn't even snap onto classic LEGO bricks.**

Chasing unrelated fads, designers commissioned custom molds freely. Within a few years, LEGO's unique parts catalog exploded from roughly **6,000** elements to **13,000–14,000**. Every oddball shape demanded another expensive custom steel mold. Supply-chain complexity spun out of control, while shoppers staring at LEGO-branded watches and non-interlocking plastic figures felt baffled: *"Is this even LEGO anymore?"*

The turnaround CEO pulled the company back from the cliff with ruthless subtraction. He sold off money-losing theme parks, shut down off-core ventures, slashed unique elements from 14,000 back down to **6,500–7,000**, and laid down one immovable rule: **every future product had to play inside the system of the classic interlocking brick.**

### The Playbook (2026): 330+ New Sets in Six Months—Yet More LEGO Than Ever

Fast-forward to late August 2026 and LEGO's **H1 2026** earnings report. In a soft global toy market, half-year revenue surged **21%** to a record **DKK 41.9 billion (about $6.53 billion)**, consumer sales jumped **22%**, and net profit soared **32%**.

Even more telling: in those six months alone, LEGO rolled out **more than 330 new sets**, with breakout hits spanning **Botanicals** (buildable adult flower bouquets), **Formula 1**, **FIFA World Cup 2026**, **Pokémon**, Star Wars, and retro home décor.

Pause for a second on that contrast. Why did branching into jewelry and action figures nearly bankrupt LEGO in 2003, while branching into forever-flowers, F1 race cars, and adult living-room décor in 2026 made LEGO bigger, pricier, and *more* unmistakably LEGO?

The answer comes down to one dead-simple expansion rule:

* **In 2003, LEGO expanded into unrelated product categories and threw away its core product language.** Watches, apparel, and snapless figures broke the mental anchor customers trusted.
* **In 2026, LEGO locked down its unmistakable core product language—the exact precision-molded, hands-on interlocking brick—while aggressively expanding into new life occasions and reasons to buy.**

Years ago, you only thought of LEGO when a kid's birthday came around. Today, Valentine's roses that never wilt, two quiet hours of Friday-night unwinding, or an F1 display piece for your desk—all became reasons to buy LEGO.

**Reasons to buy multiplied tenfold, average tickets climbed multiples higher, yet the moment you open the box, it is 100% pure LEGO.**

That rule hits close to home for every growing consumer brand. Whether you build portable power stations, kitchen appliances, or outdoor gear, the temptation is always the same: the moment a flagship SKU takes off, someone pitches slapping your logo onto unrelated white-label accessories until your store starts resembling a general-merchandise catalog. R&D gets stretched thin, and loyal buyers sense the brand going cheap. Disciplined expansion works the opposite way: **take the craft and signature design language only you do well, and apply it to a fresh everyday moment for the people who already trust you—or the neighbors standing right beside them.**

## 4. The Operator's Rulebook: Four Tests Before You Expand or Pull Back

Building a brand with pricing power isn't a coin flip between "go big" and "stay small." It comes down to knowing **where to open the throttle and where to hold the line**:

* **Open the throttle on**: real-world buying occasions that put you top of mind, and curated retail doors that let shoppers see and feel why your product is worth full price.
* **Hold the line against**: price-cutting retailers and stuffed wholesale pipelines, habit-forming markdowns that train shoppers to wait for a deal, and trend-chasing white-label side quests far outside your core craft.

The next time your team brings a new retail pitch or annual product-expansion deck for sign-off, skip the 50 glossy slides and put the plan through these four blunt questions:

> **1. The Shelf-Reach Test:** Will this new channel put us in front of genuine first-time buyers who would never find our website—and let them experience the product firsthand? Or will it simply divert existing full-price website buyers over to hunt for coupons?
>
> **2. The Full-Price Firewall Test:** If this partner starts slashing prices to dump stock tomorrow, do we have contract terms, shipment pacing, or distinct product tiers ready to shut the valve immediately?
>
> **3. The Unbranded Proof Test:** Peel our logo completely off the product. Looking solely at the engineering, materials, feel, or warranty, why would a shopper gladly pay 30% or more above a generic alternative at full price?
>
> **4. The Craft-Boundary Test:** Does this new product apply what we do better than anyone else to unlock a fresh use occasion—or are we just chasing someone else's hot trend with a private-label sticker?

Nike paid billions in market-value whiplash to relearn respect for physical shelves and heritage scarcity. On Running traded a single quarter of easy wholesale shipments for a 66.9% underlying gross margin. LEGO came back from the brink to build two decades of compounding growth by innovating strictly inside the brick.

Strip away the noise, and every durable brand draws its discipline from the exact same place: **knowing which dollar belongs to you—and having the backbone to walk away from the quick dollar that doesn't.**
